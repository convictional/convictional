<p>Professor Koenig,</p>

<p>I hope you're well. I have an ethics question I've been sitting with and you're the first person I thought to ask.</p>

<p>We're deciding whether to ship an AI feature that suggests redlines during contract review — specifically, it would propose edits based on the lawyer's own prior redlines and the firm's playbook. The lawyer sees the suggestion, accepts or rejects it, and has full visibility into why it was suggested.</p>

<p>The feature is genuinely useful. But I keep coming back to a specific scenario: a junior associate, working under time pressure, accepting AI-suggested redlines without the depth of analysis they'd apply if they were writing the edit themselves. Not because the AI is wrong — but because the AI's framing shapes the associative's cognitive engagement with the clause. Is there a professional responsibility argument that this kind of assistive technology changes the standard of care analysis, even if the lawyer technically reviews and approves each suggestion?</p>

<p>I'm not asking for a legal opinion — just a well-reasoned perspective from someone who thinks about this carefully. I'm trying to make sure we're building something that genuinely helps lawyers rather than something that creates a new category of malpractice risk they don't fully see.</p>

<p>Maren</p>
