[Darren — Panel 1]: Priyanka, thanks for coming in. Maren and I have been looking forward to this. Before we get into the scorecard, tell us about Gradel. What was that role?

[Priyanka]: Gradel was a Series B infrastructure company when I joined. Fifty people. I was the fourth operations hire — they hired me as "Chief of Staff to the CEO" but the first month I realized the title was a promise the company didn't know how to keep. The CEO, Joss, had been running the exec meetings himself, writing the board updates himself, doing the candidate debriefs himself. My job was to make each of those someone else's default output with his edits, and to free up about fifteen hours of his week. I did that over the course of about four months.

[Maren]: What was the hardest part?

[Priyanka]: Getting Joss to actually hand things off. He wanted to edit every board update for the first two months, which was fine — I wanted him to edit them, not write them. But there was a phase around month three where he kept pulling pieces of the exec meeting prep back because he thought I was "doing it differently than he would." I had to sit down with him and say "you can either tell me the rules you want me to follow, or you can trust me to develop rules that work. You can't keep changing the rules on Thursday." That was a hard conversation.

[Darren]: What did he do?

[Priyanka]: He laughed, said I was right, and then did it twice more over the next six weeks. The third time I wrote down the rules I thought I was operating with, sent them to him, and asked him to approve or edit. He edited one. After that we had a shared reference point.

[Maren]: That's good. Let me ask a version of that question. What would you do in your first thirty days at Ellery if you got the role?

[Priyanka]: I'd want to understand Darren's week in specific detail before I change anything. Ninety-minute calendar audit in the first week — not just looking at meetings but asking which ones he wishes were shorter, which ones he dreads, which ones he wishes had a different set of participants. From that I'd propose three to five changes. I wouldn't make them unilaterally — I'd send them as a memo and ask for a yes or no on each.

[Darren]: What's the thing you'd be looking for in the audit?

[Priyanka]: Coordination overhead that has calcified. I've found that founder calendars accumulate meetings that used to matter. A one-on-one that was great when someone was new and stopped being useful a year in. A recurring forecast review that doubled up with something else. The CEO is usually the last person to notice.

[Darren]: You just described my Tuesday.

[Maren]: Priyanka, let me ask a different question. We have a roadmap tension right now — an AI-forward feature bet versus closing coverage gaps. You don't need to weigh in on the substance, but if you were in the room, what's the role you'd want to play in helping us decide?

[Priyanka]: I'd want to be the person making sure each of you has written your position in a form the others can react to. I'd also want to be the person who names the tradeoff if nobody else will. That's the part of the role I have strong opinions about — a good CoS names the thing in the room that everyone is dancing around. They don't decide it. But they don't let the meeting end with the thing still undiscussed.

[Maren]: Good answer.

[Priya — Panel 2]: Priyanka, short one. Can you show me something you wrote at Gradel? A memo, a post-mortem, a planning doc — whatever you're most proud of.

[Priyanka]: I sent the one-page "how exec meetings work" doc to the recruiter last week, did she forward it?

[Priya]: Yes. I've read it. I just want to hear you talk about it.

[Priyanka]: The doc is the rule set I wrote for Joss after our month-three conversation. Five bullets: who owns the agenda, who owns the pre-read, how decisions are logged, what happens when someone can't make it, and what a "decision meeting" is versus an "update meeting." The fifth one was the most important. We were conflating them and Joss thought every meeting was a decision meeting, which meant nobody came prepared to decide.

[Priya]: What would you do differently at Ellery?

[Priyanka]: I'd want to understand Priya — sorry, the CTO's — cadence before I touch engineering meetings. I've found that engineering teams have rhythms that work for them and the CoS job is to not break them.

[Priya]: Thank you. That's the right answer.

[Tessa — Panel 3]: Priyanka, last panel. Tell me a time you had to tell a sales leader "no."

[Priyanka]: At Gradel, the head of sales wanted Joss on every pricing call for deals over a certain size. I agreed with him that Joss should be on the biggest deals, but he was defining "biggest" in a way that pulled Joss into about four calls a week. I told him no, and I proposed a different rule — Joss on the top three deals at any given time, named by the sales leader, reviewed monthly. He pushed back. I held the line. He came around when the first month played out and his top three got the attention they needed.

[Tessa]: Good. One more — Mateo on my team is going to want Darren on some portion of the Keating work. How do you decide which portion?

[Priyanka]: I'd ask Mateo what specifically Darren changes when he's in the room — is it authority, is it technical depth, is it narrative? If it's narrative, I'd write the narrative and send it to Mateo. If it's authority, Darren comes. If it's technical, we route to Priya. I'd want to be the person asking the "what's the actual ask" question before the meeting gets on the calendar.

[Tessa]: You're going to do well here. I hope we get to work together.
